Kitchen Volcano

Chemistry Ages 5+

What You'll Need

  • Baking soda (2 tablespoons)
  • White vinegar (½ cup)
  • Dish soap (a small squirt)
  • Food coloring (red or orange, optional)
  • A cup, jar, or plastic bottle
  • A tray or baking sheet to catch overflow

Instructions

  1. Place your cup or bottle on the tray so cleanup is easy.
  2. Add 2 tablespoons of baking soda into the container.
  3. Squirt in a small amount of dish soap and add a few drops of food coloring.
  4. Slowly pour the vinegar into the container and step back!
  5. Watch the fizzy "lava" erupt over the edges. Try different vinegar amounts and observe the changes.

The Science: Baking soda (a base) reacts with vinegar (an acid) to produce carbon dioxide gas. The dish soap traps the gas in bubbles, making the eruption foamy and dramatic — a classic acid-base reaction!

Unbreakable Egg Bridge

Engineering Ages 7+

What You'll Need

  • 4 raw eggs
  • Masking tape
  • A flat, lightweight board or hardcover book
  • Small canned goods for weight

Instructions

  1. Wrap a small piece of tape around the middle of each egg to reinforce them slightly.
  2. Arrange the 4 eggs in a rectangular pattern, pointed ends facing up, on a flat surface.
  3. Gently rest the board on top of the four eggs so weight distributes evenly.
  4. Slowly begin stacking canned goods on top of the board, one at a time.
  5. Keep count! See how many cans the eggs can hold before they crack. Most people are shocked by the result.

The Science: An egg's dome shape distributes weight evenly across its curved surface — the same principle behind arches in architecture. This is called a compressive structure, and it's why domes and arches can support tremendous loads.

Walking Water Rainbow

Physics Ages 5+

What You'll Need

  • 7 clear cups or glasses
  • Paper towels (3–4 sheets, folded lengthwise)
  • Water
  • Food coloring — red, yellow, blue

Instructions

  1. Line up 7 cups in a row. Fill cups 1, 3, 5, and 7 about ¾ full with water.
  2. Add red food coloring to cups 1 and 7, yellow to cup 3, and blue to cup 5.
  3. Fold paper towels into strips about 1 inch wide, and drape one strip from cup 1 into cup 2, another from cup 2 into cup 3, and so on — connecting all seven cups.
  4. Wait and watch! Over 1–2 hours, the colored water will "walk" up and over the paper towels into the empty cups.
  5. The empty cups will fill with new, blended colors — orange, green, and purple — creating a full rainbow.

The Science: Water molecules climb through tiny gaps in the paper towel fibers through a process called capillary action. This is the same way plants pull water from their roots up to their leaves!

Homemade Compass

Earth Science Ages 8+

What You'll Need

  • A sewing needle
  • A small magnet (a refrigerator magnet works)
  • A small piece of cork or foam (about 1 inch)
  • A shallow dish of water

Instructions

  1. Rub the magnet along the needle about 30–50 times in the same direction (not back and forth). This magnetizes the needle.
  2. Carefully push the needle through the center of the cork or foam piece so it lies flat.
  3. Fill a shallow dish with water and gently place the cork with the needle on the water's surface.
  4. Watch as the needle slowly rotates and settles. It will align itself along the north–south axis.
  5. Use a real compass or a compass app on a phone to verify your homemade compass is pointing north!

The Science: Rubbing the magnet on the needle aligns its iron atoms, turning it into a tiny magnet. Earth itself has a magnetic field, and the magnetized needle aligns with it — just like a real compass needle does.

Balloon Rocket Racer

Physics Ages 5+

What You'll Need

  • A balloon (long ones work best)
  • A long piece of string or fishing line (10–15 feet)
  • A plastic straw
  • Tape
  • Two chairs or posts to tie the string between

Instructions

  1. Thread the string through the straw, then tie each end of the string to a chair. Pull the chairs apart so the string is taut.
  2. Blow up the balloon and pinch the end shut — don't tie it.
  3. While pinching, tape the inflated balloon to the straw (tape the straw along the top of the balloon).
  4. Slide the straw-balloon to one end of the string, then let go!
  5. The balloon will rocket across the string. Try different balloon sizes and string angles to see what changes the speed and distance.

The Science: This demonstrates Newton's Third Law — for every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. Air rushes backward out of the balloon, pushing it forward along the string. Real rockets work the same way, but with burning fuel instead of air!

Build a Model Lung

Anatomy Ages 7+

What You'll Need

  • A plastic bottle (16 oz, cut in half — use the top half)
  • 2 small balloons
  • 1 larger balloon or a piece of a plastic bag
  • A rubber band
  • A Y-shaped straw or 2 straws taped together
  • Modeling clay or tape to seal edges

Instructions

  1. Attach a small balloon to each branch of the Y-straw. These represent the lungs.
  2. Push the straw through the bottle's mouth so the balloons hang inside the bottle.
  3. Seal around the straw at the bottle opening with clay so no air can leak.
  4. Stretch the large balloon (or plastic) over the open bottom of the bottle and secure with a rubber band. This is your "diaphragm."
  5. Pull the diaphragm down and watch the lung balloons inflate. Push it up and they deflate. You've built a working respiratory model!

The Science: When you pull the diaphragm down, it increases the volume inside the bottle and decreases the air pressure, causing air to rush in and inflate the "lungs." This is exactly how your real diaphragm muscle helps you breathe.

Germ Spread Experiment

Hygiene Ages 5+

What You'll Need

  • Glitter (fine craft glitter works best)
  • Hand lotion or cooking oil
  • Soap and water
  • Paper towels

Instructions

  1. Rub a thin layer of lotion or oil on your hands, then sprinkle glitter generously over both palms and fingers.
  2. Now go about normal activities for 5 minutes — touch doorknobs, books, your face, shake hands with a family member.
  3. Stop and look around. Notice how much glitter has spread to surfaces and other people. Each speck represents a "germ."
  4. Try wiping your hands with just a dry paper towel. How much glitter remains?
  5. Now wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water for 20 seconds. Compare the result — the soap breaks the oil and washes the "germs" away.

The Science: Germs (bacteria and viruses) spread through touch just like glitter does. Soap works by breaking down the oils that germs cling to and creating a slippery surface that rinses them away. Twenty seconds of scrubbing is the sweet spot for removing most pathogens.

Bag Greenhouse Seed Sprout

Botany Ages 5+

What You'll Need

  • A clear ziplock bag
  • A damp paper towel
  • 2–3 dried beans (lima, pinto, or kidney)
  • Tape
  • A sunny window

Instructions

  1. Dampen a paper towel so it's wet but not dripping, then fold it to fit inside the ziplock bag.
  2. Place 2–3 dried beans on top of the paper towel, spacing them apart, and seal the bag.
  3. Tape the bag to a sunny window at the child's eye level.
  4. Check daily! Mist or re-dampen the towel if it dries out. Within 3–7 days, roots and a tiny shoot will emerge.
  5. Track the growth by drawing the bean each day in a journal. Once the sprout has leaves, transplant it into soil if desired.

The Science: Seeds contain a tiny embryo plant plus stored food. Given water, warmth, and light, the seed "wakes up" through a process called germination. The root grows down (gravitropism) and the shoot grows up toward light (phototropism).

DIY Cheek Cell Observation

Cell Biology Ages 8+

What You'll Need

  • A clean toothpick or cotton swab
  • A glass of water
  • A drop of food coloring (blue works best)
  • A white plate or shallow bowl
  • A magnifying glass (or phone camera zoomed in)

Instructions

  1. Gently scrape the inside of your cheek with a clean toothpick or the flat side of a cotton swab. This collects cheek cells (it doesn't hurt!).
  2. Swirl the toothpick in a small amount of water on the white plate to transfer the cells.
  3. Add one drop of food coloring to the water. The dye will stain the cells, making them easier to see.
  4. Use a magnifying glass (or your phone's camera on max zoom) to examine the plate. Look for tiny, flat, oval shapes — those are your cheek cells.
  5. Compare what you see to pictures of animal cells online. Can you identify the cell membrane and the darker nucleus in the center?

The Science: Every part of your body is made up of trillions of microscopic cells. Cheek cells are flat epithelial cells that line the inside of your mouth. Each one has a membrane (outer wall), cytoplasm (jelly-like interior), and a nucleus (the "brain" that holds your DNA).

Measure Your Heart Rate

Physiology Ages 6+

What You'll Need

  • A timer or stopwatch (phone works fine)
  • Paper and pencil to record results
  • Your own two fingers (index and middle)

Instructions

  1. Sit still and relaxed for 2 minutes. Then place your index and middle fingers on the inside of your wrist (below the thumb) until you feel a pulse.
  2. Start the timer and count each beat for 30 seconds. Multiply that number by 2 to get your resting heart rate (beats per minute).
  3. Write down your resting heart rate. Now do 1 minute of jumping jacks, running in place, or dancing.
  4. Immediately re-measure your pulse for 30 seconds and multiply by 2 again. Record this active heart rate.
  5. Rest for 5 minutes and measure one more time. Compare all three numbers. How long did it take your heart to return to its resting rate?

The Science: Your heart pumps blood carrying oxygen to every muscle in your body. When you exercise, your muscles need more oxygen, so your heart beats faster to deliver it. A healthy heart recovers quickly back to its resting rate — this is called cardiovascular fitness.
